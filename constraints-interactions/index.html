    Constraints and Interactions

    Constraints and interactions control how parts in your assembly relate to each other. Choosing the right one affects both how loads are transmitted and how the solver handles degrees of freedom.

    What types of constraints are available in Abaqus?

    Tie Constraint

    A tie constraint bonds two surfaces together — nodes on the slave surface are forced to follow the master surface with no relative motion. It is the most common constraint for connecting parts with incompatible meshes (different element sizes or types at the interface).

    • Use when: parts are permanently bonded (adhesive joints, welded flanges, skin-to-spar connections)
    • The mesh does not need to match at the interface
    • No sliding or separation is allowed

    How to create a tie constraint: In the Interaction Module, go to Constraint > Create, select Tie, define the master and slave surfaces, and click OK.

    Rigid Body Constraint

    A rigid body constraint forces a set of nodes to move as a single rigid object — all nodes in the set maintain their relative positions. This is the standard way to apply loads or boundary conditions to a distributed region through a single Reference Point (RP).

    • Use when: you want to apply a load or BC to an entire face, edge, or region through one control point
    • The constrained region is treated as infinitely stiff (no deformation within it)
    • Simplifies the DOF count significantly — instead of solving for every node independently, Abaqus only tracks the RP

    Why it matters for loading: Rather than applying a load to hundreds of individual nodes, you define a rigid body, then apply your total load or moment directly to the RP. The rigid body distributes it to all the attached nodes automatically.

    How to create a rigid body: 1. In the Interaction Module, go to Constraint > Create 2. Select Rigid Body 3. Select the region to be made rigid (faces, cells, or node sets) 4. Select or create a Reference Point — this is the control node for the rigid region 5. Click OK

    To apply loads to the RP: in the Load Module, create a concentrated force or moment and select the RP as the target.

    Coupling Constraint

    A coupling constraint links a reference point to a surface but allows you to choose which degrees of freedom are coupled. There are two types:

    Kinematic coupling — constrains all selected DOFs of the surface nodes to the RP. Behaves similarly to a rigid body but is more flexible about which DOFs are coupled.

    Distributing coupling — distributes loads from the RP to the surface nodes in a weighted average. The surface can still deform; it is not fully rigid.

    • Use kinematic coupling when you want rigid-body-like behavior but need to leave some DOFs free
    • Use distributing coupling when you want to apply a resultant force/moment to a region while allowing the surface to flex

    MPC (Multi-Point Constraint)

    MPCs are user-defined equations that relate the displacement of one node to another. They are the most flexible option but also the most complex. Common built-in MPC types include:

    • PIN — connects two nodes with all translational DOFs tied (no rotational coupling)
    • BEAM — connects two nodes with all translational and rotational DOFs tied
    • TIE — ties all DOFs (equivalent to a tie constraint between nodes)

    For most capstone applications, tie constraints or rigid bodies are sufficient and simpler to set up.

    Constraint comparison

    Constraint Mesh compatible? Allows deformation? Best for
    Tie No (auto-interpolates) Yes (outside constraint) Bonded part interfaces
    Rigid Body Any No (region is rigid) Applying loads via RP
    Kinematic Coupling Any No (coupled DOFs fixed) Partial DOF control at RP
    Distributing Coupling Any Yes (surface can flex) Resultant load with flexibility
    MPC Node-to-node Depends on type Custom kinematic relationships

    How do I define contact between parts?

    Contact is defined in the Interactions Module and requires two steps.

    Step 1 — Define the contact property:

    1. Go to the Interactions Module
    2. Click Interaction > Property > Create and select Contact
    3. Click Mechanical and add Tangential Behavior
    4. Choose the Lagrange friction formulation and enter the coefficient of friction

    Step 2 — Define the surfaces:

    1. Click Interaction > Create and choose Surface-to-Surface Contact
    2. Define the Master Surface and the Slave Surface
    3. Master surface nodes can penetrate the slave surface; slave nodes cannot penetrate the master
    4. Choose the stiffer or coarser-meshed surface as the master
    5. Select the sliding formulation:
    6. Finite sliding — allows arbitrary separation, sliding, and rotation (most general)
    7. Small sliding — limits contact to a small portion of the master surface; requires NLGEOM to be enabled

    For most 3D structural models, small sliding is the standard choice.

    How does a rigid body simplify the problem?

    Without a rigid body, applying a resultant load to a face means either applying individual nodal loads (tedious and mesh-dependent) or using a distributed load with a complex magnitude expression. A rigid body solves this cleanly:

    1. The entire face is grouped into one rigid set
    2. A single RP represents the group
    3. Forces and moments are applied to the RP — Abaqus handles the distribution internally
    4. Reaction forces and moments at the RP are easy to extract

    The trade-off is that the constrained region cannot deform. For load introduction zones where you expect the real structure to flex, consider distributing coupling instead.
